Learning Italian for Study and Work in Italy: A Beginner's Guide for International Students

By the Primegate Consultancy Advisory Team · Last updated 17 October 2026 · 4 min read

A guide for international students and their families, from Primegate Consultancy, the appointed Exclusive Representative Office for Fondazione Doria.

You don't need to speak Italian to start a vocational programme at Fondazione Doria. Programmes are available in English and no IELTS is required. But a little Italian goes a long way: it helps you settle in, work well with your internship team and stand out to employers. This guide explains what the language levels mean, how Italian fits into your programme, and how to make a head start.

Why Italian matters for your course, internship and career

Italian isn't a barrier to entry at Fondazione Doria, but it is a real career advantage once you're in Italy, at work and for your long-term plans.

  • On your programme: training is available in English, and Italian classes help you follow everyday conversations in class and on campus.
  • At your internship: basic Italian lets you talk with colleagues and customers. The Council of Europe describes A2 learners as able to handle routine exchanges about work, shopping and local life [1].
  • For your future in Italy: Italian becomes formally important later. The EU long-term residence permit requires A2-level Italian [2], and Italian citizenship requires at least B1 [3].

What the language levels mean (A1, A2, B1) in everyday life and at work

Italian levels follow the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR). A1 and A2 are "Basic User" levels, and B1 is the first "Independent User" level [1].

LevelOfficial CEFR description (summary) [1]In everyday life and at work
A1 ("Contact") [3]Understands and uses familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases. Can introduce yourself and answer simple personal questions if people speak slowly and clearly.Greeting colleagues, introducing yourself, ordering food, asking for directions
A2 ("Survival") [3]Understands frequently used expressions about personal information, shopping, local geography and employment. Handles simple, routine exchanges.Following simple workplace instructions, shopping, using transport, routine chats with customers
B1 ("Minimum") [3]Understands the main points of clear standard language about work, school and leisure. Deals with most travel situations and can describe experiences and plans.Taking part in team conversations, explaining what you did on a shift, handling most day-to-day situations independently

How the Italian language pathway works on vocational courses

At Fondazione Doria, your training is available in English, and Italian classes from A1 to A2 level run alongside it, so you build the language as you build your skills.

Here's what that means for you:

  1. No Italian needed to apply. Programmes are available for international students in English, and no IELTS is required.
  2. No language certificate for the visa. Italian embassy checklists for vocational training visas do not list a language certificate [4].
  3. Italian classes included. You study Italian from beginner (A1) to A2 level, focused on the language you need for your educational and professional environment.
  4. Practise at your internship. Your professional internship of approximately three months is where your Italian really grows, in real conversations with real colleagues.

How to start learning before you travel

A few weeks of practice before you arrive makes your first days in Italy easier and gives you a head start in class.

Practical ways to begin:

  • Learn survival phrases first: greetings, numbers, days and times, directions, and food and shopping vocabulary. These are the A1 topics you'll use on day one [1].
  • Make it daily: short, regular practice works better than occasional long sessions.
  • Listen to Italian every day through music, podcasts, films with subtitles or news for learners.
  • Learn your trade's vocabulary. If you're joining a hospitality, healthcare, construction or IT programme, learn the names of tools, equipment and tasks in Italian.
  • Use official practice materials. The universities that run the CILS and CELI exams publish level-based materials that help you check your progress [3][5].
  • Talk to your Primegate Consultancy advisor about what to focus on before your intake month.

Italian language certificates (CELI, CILS) and when you need one

You don't need an Italian certificate to join a Fondazione Doria programme, but a recognised certificate is useful later, for example A2 for an EU long-term residence permit or B1 for citizenship [3][2].

Italy's main certificates are issued by universities and institutions that work under agreements with the Italian Ministry of Foreign Affairs [3]:

Tips for learners from any language background

Whatever your first language, the fastest way to make progress is daily practice built around real situations you'll face in Italy.

  • Lean on what you know. If you already speak English or another European language, look for words that are similar in Italian, but check them, because some look alike and mean something different.
  • Master pronunciation early. Learn how each letter and letter combination sounds in your first weeks. It makes reading and listening much easier later.
  • Practise speaking from week one. Don't wait to be "ready". Use simple sentences with classmates, at the shops and at your internship.
  • Keep a work phrasebook. Note the phrases your trainers and supervisors use most and review them daily.
  • Set a level goal. Aim for A2 by the end of your programme, then decide whether a CELI or CILS certificate would help your next step.
